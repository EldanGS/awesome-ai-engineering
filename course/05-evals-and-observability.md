# Domain 5: Evals, Testing and Observability

**Weight: 12%.** Core question: *how do we prove it works, and keep proving it after go-live?*

Evals are the solutions engineer's most persuasive asset. "It looked good in the demo" loses to a competitor who says "on 150 of your real tickets we were correct 91% of the time, here are the 13 failures and what we will do about them". Evals also protect you after the sale: they catch regressions when prompts, models or data change, and observability shows what users actually do.

## Learning objectives

- [ ] Turn customer success criteria into measurable eval metrics with thresholds.
- [ ] Build an eval dataset from real samples, edge cases and adversarial cases, with a held-out split.
- [ ] Choose grading methods: exact match, code checks, model-based grading (LLM as judge) and human review.
- [ ] Calibrate an LLM judge against human labels and report agreement.
- [ ] Evaluate agents on outcomes and trajectories, not just final text.
- [ ] Run evals in CI to catch regressions from prompt, model or data changes.
- [ ] Instrument an application with traces, token usage, latency, cost and user feedback.
- [ ] Present eval results honestly to a technical and an executive audience.

## Core concepts

### From success criteria to metrics

Start from what the customer said success means (Domain 6), then make it measurable.

| Customer statement | Metric | Grader | Example threshold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Answers must be correct and from our policies" | Answer correctness; faithfulness to sources | Model-based against reference, plus citation check | At least 90% correct, at most 2% unsupported claims |
| "It must route tickets to the right team" | Classification accuracy, per-class recall | Exact match | At least 95% overall, no class below 85% |
| "It should not make things up when it does not know" | Correct abstention rate | Exact match on the "not found" response | At least 90% on unanswerable questions |
| "Fast enough for agents on the phone" | p50 and p95 latency, TTFT | Instrumentation | p95 under 4 seconds |
| "Affordable at our volume" | Cost per task | Token accounting | Under 0.02 USD per ticket |
| "Safe and on-brand" | Policy violation rate; tone score | Model-based with a rubric plus human sample | Zero critical violations in the red-team set |

Good success criteria are **specific, measurable, achievable and relevant**. "Better customer experience" is not measurable; "first-contact resolution up from 62% to 70% on billing tickets" is.

### Building the dataset

| Source | Value | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real historical cases from the customer | Most representative | Needs data agreement and PII handling. |
| Subject-matter expert written cases | Covers rare but important cases | Experts write "clean" questions; add messy ones. |
| Production logs (after launch) | Shows real usage drift | Sample and label continuously. |
| Synthetic cases generated by a model | Fast coverage of variations | Must be reviewed; can be unrealistic or too easy. |
| Adversarial and red-team cases | Tests safety and robustness | Keep separate so they do not skew quality metrics. |

Practical rules:

- 30 to 50 cases are enough to start a POC; grow to a few hundred for production decisions.
- Label each case with expected output or grading notes, category, difficulty and source.
- Keep a **held-out** set you do not look at while tuning prompts, so you do not overfit.
- Include the cases that worry the customer most. If their compliance lead fears one scenario, it must be in the set.

### Grading methods

| Method | Good for | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exact or set match | Labels, IDs, routing, yes or no | Cheap, deterministic | Only for closed outputs. |
| Code-based checks | JSON validity, required fields, regex, numeric tolerance, citations present | Deterministic, fast | Cannot judge meaning. |
| Similarity metrics | Paraphrase-tolerant comparison | Cheap | Weakly correlated with correctness. |
| Model-based grading (LLM as judge) | Correctness against a reference, faithfulness, tone, rubric compliance | Scales to open-ended output | Bias, inconsistency; needs calibration. |
| Pairwise comparison | Choosing between two prompts or models | More reliable than absolute scores | Does not give an absolute quality level. |
| Human review | Final sign-off, calibration, subjective quality | Ground truth for nuance | Slow and costly; reviewers disagree too. |

### Making LLM-as-judge trustworthy

1. Write a specific rubric with discrete outcomes (for example `correct`, `partially_correct`, `incorrect`, `unsupported`) rather than a 1 to 10 score.
2. Give the judge the question, the reference answer or source passages, and the candidate answer.
3. Ask for a short reason before the verdict, and return a structured output.
4. Use a capable model as judge, ideally different from the one being tested, and keep its prompt versioned.
5. Label 50 to 100 cases by hand and measure agreement between judge and humans. Iterate the rubric until agreement is high enough for the decision at stake, and report it.
6. Watch for known biases: preference for longer answers, for the first option in pairwise tests, and for the judge's own style.

```python
from typing import Literal
from pydantic import BaseModel
import anthropic

class Verdict(BaseModel):
    reasoning: str
    verdict: Literal["correct", "partially_correct", "incorrect", "unsupported"]

JUDGE_PROMPT = """You grade answers from an HR policy assistant.
<question>{question}</question>
<reference>{reference}</reference>
<sources>{sources}</sources>
<answer>{answer}</answer>
Rules: "correct" means it matches the reference in substance. "unsupported" means it states
facts not present in the sources, even if they sound plausible. Explain briefly, then decide."""

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

def judge(case, answer) -> Verdict:
    response = client.messages.parse(
        model="claude-opus-5-5",
        max_tokens=1024,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": JUDGE_PROMPT.format(answer=answer, **case)}],
        output_format=Verdict,
    )
    return response.parsed_output
```

### Evaluating agents

Agents need more than a final-answer check.

| Dimension | What to measure |
| --- | --- |
| Outcome | Did the task end in the correct state (ticket updated, refund issued correctly, report contains required facts)? Check the system state, not just the text. |
| Trajectory | Were the right tools called with valid arguments, in a sensible order, without forbidden actions? |
| Efficiency | Steps, tool calls, tokens, latency and cost per task. |
| Safety | Did it ask for approval where required, resist injected instructions, stay within scope? |
| Robustness | Pass rate across repeated runs (agents are non-deterministic; run each case several times and report the pass rate). |

### Regression testing and CI

Treat prompts, tool definitions, retrieval settings and model versions as code. On every change:

1. Run the fast eval subset (deterministic checks plus a sample of judged cases) in CI.
2. Block the merge if a key metric drops beyond a tolerance or any critical safety case fails.
3. Run the full suite nightly or before releases, and before switching model versions.

Tools such as promptfoo, DeepEval, Inspect, OpenAI Evals, LangSmith and Langfuse support this workflow. A plain Python script plus a CSV is also fine for a POC; consistency matters more than tooling.

### Observability in production

| Signal | Why it matters | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traces of every request, including retrieval, tool calls and model calls | Debug any individual answer the customer asks about | OpenTelemetry spans, or an LLM observability platform |
| Token usage and cost per request, user, feature and tenant | Budget control and pricing | Read `usage` fields from each API response |
| Latency (TTFT, total, per step) | User experience and SLA tracking | Spans and percentiles |
| Errors, refusals, truncations, rate-limit events | Reliability | Structured logs and alerts |
| User feedback (thumbs, edits, escalations) | Real-world quality signal | Capture in the UI and link to traces |
| Drift in question topics and retrieval scores | Early warning of degraded quality | Periodic sampling, clustering and labelling |

OpenTelemetry's generative AI semantic conventions define standard attribute names for model calls (model, token counts, operation), so traces work across vendors. Agree with the customer up front which content is logged: prompts and responses can contain personal data, so apply redaction, retention limits and access control to traces (Domain 8).

### Presenting results

| Audience | What they need |
| --- | --- |
| Executive sponsor | Two or three headline metrics against the agreed success criteria, cost per task, top risks and the go or no-go recommendation. |
| Technical evaluators | Dataset description, grading method and judge agreement, per-category results, failure analysis, reproducible scripts. |
| Security and compliance | Red-team set results, data handling of eval data and traces. |

Show failures openly with their root cause and planned fix. Hiding them destroys trust the first time a pilot user finds one.

## Patterns and anti-patterns

| Pattern | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| Eval set agreed with the customer before building | Prevents moving goalposts at the end of a POC. |
| Error analysis before metric chasing | Reading 50 failures shows what to fix; aggregate scores do not. |
| Separate retrieval, generation and end-to-end metrics | Pinpoints the failing layer. |
| Judge calibrated against human labels | Results stand up to sceptical reviewers. |
| Traces linked to feedback | Every complaint becomes a reproducible test case. |
| Failing production cases added to the eval set | The suite grows with real usage. |

| Anti-pattern | What goes wrong |
| --- | --- |
| "Vibe checks" on five hand-picked questions | Overconfidence; failures appear in the pilot. |
| Tuning on the same cases you report | Inflated scores that do not generalise. |
| Generic benchmark scores as POC evidence | Not the customer's task or data. |
| Unvalidated 1 to 10 judge scores | Noisy numbers nobody can interpret. |
| Logging full prompts with personal data indefinitely | Privacy and compliance exposure. |
| Single-run agent results | Non-determinism hides flaky behaviour. |

## Hands-on lab: eval harness and readout

**Goal:** build a reusable eval harness for the RAG assistant from Domain 3 (or the agent from Domain 4) and present the results as you would to a customer.

1. Write down four success criteria as if agreed with a customer, each with a metric and threshold (correctness, abstention, latency, cost).
2. Assemble 80 cases: 50 representative, 15 edge cases, 10 unanswerable, 5 adversarial. Split 60 for development and 20 held out.
3. Implement graders: exact match for abstention, a code check for citation presence and validity, and an LLM judge with a discrete rubric for correctness.
4. Hand-label 40 development cases. Measure judge agreement with your labels, revise the rubric once, and measure again.
5. Instrument the application with traces (Langfuse, Phoenix or OpenTelemetry to a local collector) including token usage and latency per step.
6. Run the full suite on two configurations (for example two models or two retrieval settings). Run agent cases three times each.
7. Add the fast subset to a CI workflow (GitHub Actions or similar) that fails if correctness drops more than three points.
8. Perform error analysis on all failures: categorise by root cause.

**Deliverable:** `eval-report.md` with the success criteria table, dataset description, judge agreement before and after calibration, results per configuration and category, failure taxonomy with counts, held-out results, and a one-paragraph executive summary. Include a screenshot of one trace.

## Review questions

1. A champion says, "We tested it with ten questions and it was great. Can we go to production?" What do you propose?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Agree measurable success criteria and build a representative eval set (at least 30 to 50 real cases including edge, unanswerable and adversarial ones), with a held-out portion. Run it, analyse failures and report results against thresholds. Ten hand-picked questions cannot reveal failure rates or regressions.

    </details>

2. Your LLM judge scores answers 8.7 out of 10 on average. A sceptical evaluator asks what that means. How should you have designed it?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Use a rubric with discrete, defined verdicts (such as correct, partially correct, incorrect, unsupported), give the judge the reference or sources, require a short reason and structured output, and calibrate against human labels, reporting the agreement rate. Then you can say, for example, "88% correct, judge agrees with experts on 92% of cases".

    </details>

3. Why keep a held-out eval set during a POC?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Because tuning prompts and settings on the same cases you report inflates scores. A held-out set you do not inspect during tuning gives an honest estimate of performance on unseen data, which is what the customer will experience.

    </details>

4. How do you evaluate an agent that issues refunds, beyond reading its final message?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Check the outcome in the system state (correct refund amount, correct invoice, no extra actions), inspect the trajectory (right tools, valid arguments, approval requested when required, no forbidden actions), measure efficiency (steps, tokens, latency) and repeat each case several times to report a pass rate, because agent behaviour is non-deterministic.

    </details>

5. A prompt change improves correctness from 86% to 90% but one critical safety case now fails. Ship it?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    No. Critical safety cases are blocking gates, not part of an average. Fix the regression, then re-run the full suite. Encode this rule in CI so that any critical case failure blocks the merge regardless of aggregate improvement.

    </details>

6. What should production traces capture, and what privacy precautions apply?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Each step of the request (retrieval, tool calls, model calls), model and parameters, token usage, cost, latency, errors and linked user feedback. Because prompts and outputs may contain personal data, agree what content is logged, redact sensitive fields, restrict access and set retention limits in line with the customer's policies.

    </details>

7. In the POC readout, how should you present the 14 failures out of 150 cases?

    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Openly, grouped by root cause (for example retrieval misses on scanned PDFs, ambiguous questions, missing data), with examples, the planned fix for each category and the expected impact. Show the headline metrics against the agreed thresholds first for executives, with the detailed analysis available for technical evaluators.

    </details>
